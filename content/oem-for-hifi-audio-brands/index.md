## Overview

An audiophile decides how a loudspeaker sounds long before it plays a note. They run a hand along the top panel, tilt the cabinet under the showroom lights to watch the grain move, and look for the seam where two veneer leaves meet. For a hi-fi brand, the cabinet is not packaging around the drivers — it is the physical argument for the price on the tag. Woodsat builds cabinets as an OEM partner for exactly that audience: brands whose customers will scrutinise a finish for minutes before they ever discuss frequency response.

This service is deliberately different from high-volume consumer OEM work. Hi-fi programmes tend to be smaller in quantity, higher in finish expectation, and far less forgiving of variation between one pair and the next. A single visible glue line or a half-shade colour drift between the left and right cabinet is enough to sink a review and, with it, a launch. Everything below is written for brand owners, product managers and chief engineers who already know what they want their speaker to be and need a manufacturing partner who can protect that intent through hundreds of units rather than one hero sample.

## Who This Service Is For

The typical partner here is a boutique or established loudspeaker brand launching a flagship floorstander, a reference standmount, an anniversary edition or a small run of dealer demonstration pairs. Volumes commonly sit between a few dozen and a few thousand cabinets per model per year — enough to justify proper tooling and process control, but small enough that hand skill still matters on every unit. These customers usually own their acoustic design and industrial design outright and are looking for a cabinet maker, not a design house.

What unites them is a low tolerance for cosmetic and dimensional variance. The audiophile press photographs cabinets in raking light. Dealers stack matched pairs side by side on a demo wall. End customers keep these speakers for a decade or more and notice if the veneer ages unevenly. A hi-fi OEM programme therefore has to be judged on its worst unit, not its best, and that is the standard we plan production around.

## Why Hi-Fi Cabinets Are a Different Manufacturing Problem

Consumer speaker manufacturing rewards throughput; hi-fi manufacturing rewards repeatable perfection at modest quantity. The difficulty is that the two goals pull in opposite directions. A high-gloss piano finish that looks flawless on one cabinet takes multiple coats, careful flatting between coats and a controlled drying environment — and every one of those steps introduces a chance for the next cabinet to look slightly different. Our job is to remove that chance through master samples, locked coating batches and disciplined inspection rather than by hoping a skilled sprayer has a good day.

Veneer continuity is the second problem unique to this segment. A brand that sells a walnut flagship expects the walnut on unit two hundred to look like the walnut on the review sample. That is only possible if veneer is reserved from the same flitch, sequenced, and book-matched to a documented layout — decisions that have to be made at the very start of a programme, not when stock runs low mid-run. We plan flitch reservation and leaf sequencing into the quotation so the look you approve is the look you keep.

The programme opens with an NDA and a review of your cabinet drawings, reference speaker or industrial-design model. Because hi-fi customers usually arrive with a finished design, our early contribution is a manufacturability and finish-feasibility assessment: which radius is realistic in solid timber, whether a chosen veneer will wrap a tight curve without splitting, and how many gloss coats your target surface actually needs. We then build a prototype and refine it into a golden sample that becomes the physical reference for the whole run.

Once the sample is signed, we reserve veneer flitches, cut spray masks and build the fixtures that make matched-pair output repeatable. Production runs against that reference with in-process inspection at machining, pre-closure, post-finish and final assembly. Cabinets are matched into pairs — and into quads or larger sets where a customer runs matched multi-channel systems — then packed in protective, often branded, packaging designed to survive courier and dealer handling without corner damage to a gloss surface.

## Quality, Acoustics and Matching

Cabinet quality for hi-fi is measured on two axes at once: how it looks and how little it colours the sound. On the cosmetic side, every unit passes colour, gloss-level and surface-defect inspection against approved master panels under standardised lighting, and pairs are compared side by side before they are boxed. On the acoustic side, we verify the things a cabinet is responsible for — airtightness, brace placement, damping mass and port dimensions — so the enclosure your engineers voiced is the enclosure that ships.

Matching deserves its own mention because it is where hi-fi programmes most often go wrong at other suppliers. We treat a stereo pair as a single deliverable: the left and right cabinets are drawn from compatible veneer, finished in the same batch, and inspected together. For brands running matched multi-channel or multi-way systems, the same logic extends to larger sets. All coating batches, veneer flitch references and inspection results are retained against the lot so a query years later can be traced rather than guessed.

## Commercial Terms for Low-Volume Hi-Fi Programmes

The economics of a hi-fi cabinet programme are different from mainstream consumer OEM, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. At a few dozen to a few thousand units a year, tooling and fixture costs cannot be amortised across the huge quantities that make consumer pricing possible, so the honest conversation at quotation stage is about how one-time development and tooling costs are recovered — whether absorbed into unit price over a committed volume, paid as a separate development charge, or shared in some blend the two parties agree. We would rather have that conversation openly at the start than surprise a partner with it later.

Low volume also changes how a launch is scheduled. Rather than a single large production run, hi-fi programmes are often built in phased batches timed to a dealer roll-out, a show launch or a review cycle, which lets a brand manage cash and inventory instead of warehousing a full year of a premium product on day one. We plan capacity and reserve materials against that phased calendar, and we hold safety stock of the long-lead items a hi-fi build depends on — a specific veneer flitch, a custom driver gasket, a particular hardware finish — so a small follow-on batch does not stall for want of a single component.

## Protecting a Flagship Before Launch

A flagship loudspeaker is often a brand's most closely guarded project, and the manufacturing partner is one of the few outside parties who sees it before the world does. We treat that access as a responsibility. Beyond the standard NDA, unreleased programmes are handled with restricted drawing access, controlled and identified tooling, and discretion about even acknowledging that a project exists. Review-sample and press-photography units are frequently the very first cabinets built to full finish standard, and they receive particular attention because they set the first public impression of the product.

We also understand embargoes and launch timing from the manufacturing side. Finished cabinets can be held, staged and released to align with a coordinated announcement rather than leaking into the channel early, and packaging and marking can be kept generic until a launch date if a brand wants to keep a model unidentifiable in transit. These are small operational disciplines, but for a product whose entire commercial impact depends on a controlled reveal, they matter.

## Working With Your Design and Acoustic Teams

Most hi-fi partners arrive with strong opinions and completed engineering, and the best programmes run as a genuine collaboration between their designers and our production engineers rather than as a one-way instruction. Your industrial designer owns the shape and the finish intent; your acoustic engineer owns the internal architecture, bracing and tuning; we own the question of how to build that faithfully, repeatably and at the agreed cost. When those three responsibilities are clear, decisions get made quickly and nothing important falls between the parties.

In practice that means we feed manufacturability and finish observations back early — a radius that is difficult in a chosen veneer, a tolerance that is tighter than the acoustics require, a construction sequence that would improve yield — and your teams decide what to accept. Nothing that affects fit, finish or acoustics changes without a written engineering change and, where needed, re-sampling against the golden sample. The result is a cabinet that is unmistakably your design, built to a standard your reviewers and customers will reward.
