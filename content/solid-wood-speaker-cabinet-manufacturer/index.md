## Overview

Solid wood is the material customers imagine when they picture a beautiful loudspeaker, and it is also the material that punishes a careless manufacturer most severely. Unlike a panel product, a plank of hardwood is never truly finished moving. It expands and contracts across the grain with every seasonal change in humidity, and a cabinet built as though the timber were dimensionally stable will, sooner or later, develop a split panel, a cracked mitre or a finish that crazes along a glue line. Building solid-wood speaker cabinets well is therefore a woodworking discipline first and a speaker-making discipline second.

Woodsat manufactures genuine solid hardwood speaker cabinets for brands and designers who want the real material rather than a veneer over a composite core. That decision has real acoustic and structural consequences, both good and bad, and this page is honest about them. It is written for premium and luxury audio brands, boutique makers and product designers who value the natural-wood story enough to accept the higher cost, longer lead time and greater craft that solid timber demands — and who need a partner that understands how to keep it stable for the life of the product.

The buyer here is rarely chasing the lowest cost. ## The Central Challenge: Wood Movement

Everything difficult about solid-wood cabinets comes back to one fact: wood moves across the grain but barely along it, and it keeps doing so for its whole life in response to humidity. A wide solid panel can change dimension by several millimetres between a damp summer and a dry, heated winter. Glue that panel rigidly into a frame on all four edges and something has to give — usually the panel cracks or the joint fails. The craft of solid-wood construction is designing joinery that holds the cabinet square and airtight while still allowing the timber to expand and contract.

We address this the way fine furniture makers do, adapted to the airtight requirements of an acoustic enclosure. That means floating panel constructions where a solid panel sits in a groove and is free to move, breadboard and spline details that accommodate movement without opening a gap, and grain orientation planned so that movement works with the structure rather than against it. Where a design needs a large uninterrupted solid surface, we discuss the real risks openly and often propose a hybrid — solid-wood frames, edges and baffles with an engineered core where a big flat panel would otherwise be a liability.

Production starts before a single cut, with selection of the species and the individual boards. We grade stock for grain character, colour consistency and — critically — stability, setting aside boards with the kind of figure or defect that predicts trouble. Selected timber is kiln dried and then conditioned toward the humidity it will actually live in, because a cabinet built from over-dry or over-wet stock is a crack waiting to happen regardless of how well it is joined.

Boards are then jointed and grain-matched into panels, assembled with movement-tolerant joinery, and checked for squareness and airtightness before finishing. Because solid wood must breathe, the finishing philosophy differs from a gloss veneer programme: we favour penetrating oils, hardwax finishes and flexible coatings that move with the timber and can be maintained over a long life, rather than a rigid film that looks spectacular on day one and crazes as the wood works beneath it. Every cabinet is inspected for the acoustic essentials — airtight seals, correct bracing and damping — alongside its cosmetic grade.

## Solid Wood and Acoustics

Solid timber is not automatically the best-sounding cabinet material, and a good manufacturer will say so. A thick solid panel is dense and rigid, which is helpful, but solid wood also tends to ring at its own resonances more than a well-damped composite panel does. The acoustic art is to keep the desirable stiffness while controlling the ringing, which we do with internal bracing tuned to the panel dimensions, added damping where it earns its place, and wall thicknesses chosen for the design rather than copied from a veneered version of the same box.

This is why we treat a solid-wood cabinet as its own acoustic design rather than a re-skin of an existing MDF enclosure. The bracing plan, the damping mass and the panel thicknesses are all reconsidered for the way solid timber behaves. For brands that own their acoustics, we build faithfully to their bracing and damping specification; for those who want input, we contribute construction detailing that preserves the sound they are after while keeping the cabinet stable and buildable.

## Designing for Solid Wood From the Start

The most common cause of trouble in a solid-wood speaker is a design that was drawn for MDF and then simply remade in hardwood. The two materials want to be built in different ways, and a solid-wood cabinet succeeds or fails on decisions made at the drawing stage, not on the workshop floor. A large unbroken solid panel, a rigidly captured mitre, a finish film that cannot flex, grain running the wrong way across a joint — each is a latent failure that no amount of careful assembly can fully rescue. Bringing us in early, while the cabinet is still a drawing, is far more valuable than handing over a finished MDF design and asking for it in walnut.

When we engage at the design stage, the conversation is about accommodating movement gracefully within the look the brand wants. That might mean breaking a large face into framed panels that can move, orienting grain so seasonal change works with the structure, choosing joinery that stays tight and airtight as the wood works, and selecting a finish system matched to a moving substrate. None of this compromises the aesthetic; done well it is invisible to the customer, who simply owns a beautiful solid-wood speaker that stays beautiful. It is precisely the sort of manufacturability input a specialist solid-wood maker exists to provide.

## Sustainability, Certification and Responsible Sourcing

For many brands choosing solid hardwood, the natural-material story is inseparable from a sustainability story, and a customer paying a premium for real wood increasingly wants to know that wood was sourced responsibly. We can supply timber from certified and responsibly managed sources, and we keep the material documentation that lets a brand substantiate its claims rather than merely assert them. For products sold into markets or tenders with environmental requirements, that documented chain matters as much as the appearance of the wood itself.

Responsible sourcing also intersects with quality. Well-managed timber, properly graded and dried, behaves more predictably than cheap stock of unknown origin, so the sustainable choice and the durable choice frequently coincide. Where a brand has specific species, origin or certification requirements, we build those into sourcing from the start of a programme, and we are candid where a particular demand — a rare species, a specific certification, a certain figure — will affect availability, lead time or cost so the decision is made with full information.

## Built to Last: Repair and Refinishing

A solid-wood speaker is often bought as a long-term or even lifetime purchase, and one of the quiet advantages of genuine solid timber over a thin veneer is that it can be maintained and repaired rather than discarded. A surface that dulls after years of use can be re-oiled or refinished; a knock or scratch that would expose a substrate on a veneered cabinet can often be sanded and blended on solid wood. Designing and finishing with that maintainability in mind turns a cabinet into a product a customer keeps and passes on rather than replaces.

We factor longevity into how a solid-wood cabinet is built and finished. Penetrating oil and hardwax finishes are chosen partly because they can be refreshed by an owner or a dealer over the years without a full strip-back, and construction is detailed so that, where sensible, a damaged element can be addressed rather than condemning the whole cabinet. For heirloom and statement products this is part of the value proposition itself: a speaker built to be lived with for decades, ageing gracefully as solid wood does, rather than looking tired the first time it is knocked.
